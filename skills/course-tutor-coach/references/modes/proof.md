# Mode: proof

**Subjects:** discrete math, linear algebra, any course where the task is establishing that something must be true.

- **Item:** one claim to prove.
- **One rep:** a complete, valid argument the student constructed. Partial credit isn't a rep.
- **Session size:** 3–5 items. Proofs are long; a 10-item proof session isn't sustainable.

## Start here, before anything else

Ask them to state two things: **what they may assume, and what they must show.** Most stuck proofs are stuck exactly there and nowhere else — the student is trying to manipulate symbols before knowing what the target is. This single question resolves a surprising share of "I have no idea where to start."

## What bucket (c) means here

A gap in an otherwise sound argument: an unhandled case, a step that's true but unjustified, a quantifier that's wrong in the write-up though right in their head.

Distinguishing it from (b): if the *skeleton* is right and a step is unsupported, that's (c). If they chose the wrong technique for the claim's shape, that's (b) — and it's the more important one to catch, because technique selection is the transferable skill.

## The specific hazard of this mode

Most proofs have one key move that makes the whole thing work — the right induction hypothesis, the right thing to assume for contradiction, the right object to construct. **Say it and the exercise is over.** There is no partial version of giving away the key insight.

So L2 names the *technique* — induction, contradiction, contrapositive, construction — and stops. It never names the witness, the construction, or the hypothesis. That restraint is most of the value in this mode.

## Hint ladder in this mode

- **L1** — "What form does this claim have? For-all, there-exists, if-then?" (Form usually implies technique.)
- **L2** — Name the technique only.
- **L3** — Prove an analogous simpler claim by the same technique, so they see the shape.
- **L4** — Do the *setup* — write the hypothesis and the goal formally — not the key step.
