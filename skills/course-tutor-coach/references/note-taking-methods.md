# Note-taking methods and sources

Reference for the notes review defined in `study-process.md`. Read it when a review has found the same property missing more than once and a format change is worth recommending, or when a student asks why a practice is supposed to work. The source list at the bottom exists so no future session has to re-derive or re-fetch this.

## Formats are means; properties are the test

`study-process.md` checks properties and never grades format. That stands, and it stands for a real reason: the tutor never observes whether a format is being followed, only what ended up on the page.

But formats are not interchangeable, and saying "the evidence doesn't clearly separate them" overstates the case. **A format is a mechanism for producing a property.** The two-column math layout below physically forces reasoning to sit beside each step, which is the property most commonly absent from math notes. Cornell's cue column forces question-generation after class, which is retrieval practice rather than rereading.

So: evaluate properties, recommend formats. Recommend one only after a review has found the matching property missing more than once, and frame it as a means to that specific property rather than as a system to adopt wholesale.

## Cornell method

Developed by Walter Pauk, described in *How to Study in College* (10th ed., 2010). Good default for reading- and lecture-heavy material: humanities, social science, and the prose-and-vocabulary parts of science.

Structure: a narrow left cue column, a wide right notes column, and a summary strip across the bottom. During class, write only in the notes column. After class, fill the cue column with questions or keywords derived from what you wrote. To review, cover the notes column and quiz yourself against the cues.

The property it produces is retrieval practice. The cue column turns a page of notes into a self-quiz, which is what makes review something other than rereading.

The Cornell page also asserts that handwriting notes beats typing for retention. Treat that as weaker than the rest of this file: the underlying literature is more contested than the page implies, with mixed replication of the best-known study [TRAINING, not re-verified]. Worth mentioning to a student who asks, not worth pushing on one who types.

## Two-column method for math

Distinct from Cornell, and the better fit wherever the content is worked procedure rather than prose. Four steps:

1. **Prepare the page.** Divide into left and right columns, reserving five or six lines at the bottom. Head the page with the textbook section and topic.
2. **In class.** Left column: the problem worked step by step, as shown. Right column: as the instructor explains a step, write *why that step was taken*, beside the step it explains.
3. **Summarize.** At the bottom: key formulas, hints, and the day's takeaways.
4. **Review.** Use the notes while doing homework, read the steps aloud, revise anything unclear.

The mechanism is that procedure and reasoning end up physically separate but adjacent, so either can be quizzed independently: cover the right column and reconstruct the reasoning, or cover the left and reproduce the steps from the reasoning alone.

**This is the standard recommendation when a review finds worked examples recorded without any record of why each step happened, or forms recorded without conditions of application.** It addresses that property directly rather than generally.

## What to capture in a math or science lecture

1. Don't write down only what's on the board. Spoken commentary carries reasoning that is often written nowhere.
2. Capture that commentary specifically. It usually explains why a formula or approach was chosen, which is the part the board omits.
3. Flag whatever the instructor visibly emphasizes. It is disproportionately likely to appear on an exam.
4. Ask in the moment when something is unclear rather than carrying confusion forward.
5. When lost, don't stall the page. Write the key terms in the margin, leave space, fill in the reasoning later.
6. Go back over the notes as soon as possible after class, while it's still fresh enough to correct errors and fill gaps.
7. Review at intervals afterward. Math compounds, and this material is required again later in the same course.

## Science courses

Cornell fits science better than the two-column math method, since science carries more prose, vocabulary, and conceptual explanation relative to pure procedure. Two adaptations:

- Use the cue column for self-generated questions rather than bare keywords, so review is closer to a real quiz.
- Put diagrams and cause-effect chains inline rather than describing a mechanism only in sentences. A labeled sketch usually encodes a process faster and more durably than a paragraph.

## Non-STEM courses

Cornell unmodified is the right default. Where material is hierarchical rather than a flat sequence — main point, supporting points, sub-points, as in most humanities lecturing — the outline method fits better.

The post-class discipline is identical to math: a second pass soon after class to correct and fill gaps, then review at intervals. That is not a math-specific practice; it's retrieval plus spacing, and it generalizes.

## Supporting practices

**Error log.** Every problem gotten wrong goes into a running log recording what the failure actually was — arithmetic slip, wrong rule applied, misread question, conceptual gap — and the corrected approach. Reviewed before tests specifically, rather than folded silently back into regular notes. The value is that a given person's mistakes cluster into three or four recurring patterns, and a log makes the pattern visible where individual mistakes feel isolated. Generally attributed to Barbara Oakley, *A Mind for Numbers* [TRAINING, not verified].

Note the overlap and the trap: this repo's ledger already records exactly this, bucketed a/b/c/d and dated. But the ledger is maintained by the tutor, and the error log's value comes substantially from the student writing it. Don't tell a student the ledger replaces their own log; the two do different work, and treating one as a substitute for the other is the same substitution problem `study-process.md` warns about.

**Running formula and rule sheet.** Kept separate from dated class notes, organized by topic rather than by date, accumulated across the term, and used as the actual reference when stuck. This repo's `data/subjects/<subject>/_domain.md` is the same idea arrived at independently, so a student already keeping one has a head start on the domain ledger and the two should be reconciled rather than maintained twice.

**Closed-book redo.** Reading a worked solution is a different skill from reproducing one. After processing the day's notes, close the notebook and redo the same problem type from a blank page. The notes are raw material; this is the studying.

## The step almost everyone skips

Every source below that specifies a process converges here: **notes taken in class are a rough capture, not a finished product.** The learning happens in a deliberate second pass shortly after class, correcting and filling gaps and testing recall while the lecture is still recoverable from memory, then again at spaced intervals.

Skipping that second pass is the most common reason a note-taking system stops producing results even when the in-class capture was fine. When a review finds good capture and poor retention, check for this before recommending anything else.
